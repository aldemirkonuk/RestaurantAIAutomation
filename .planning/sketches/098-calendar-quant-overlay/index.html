The day-book, and the world outside it.

The founder asked for two things on the one page he kept unreservedly: the world outside — weather, prices, transport, the state of the food — drawn onto the days it will land on; and every calendar the house already keeps, brought inside so nobody has to leave. These two sheets are the design that answers it, and a third thing neither ask named but both need: the assistant that can act on a day, and the line it may not cross.

A · The month, with the world outside it

The deadline strip, the month grid carrying weather, a covers forecast and three risk marks per day, a day opened into its six registers, and the ⌘K assistant acting on that day.

Ends with the one table that is not example data: what each signal actually finds in production today. Five of six find nothing, and the design says so on the face of the cell rather than drawing a calm mark over an empty table.

B · Keeping the house inside the book

The connection register — Google, Microsoft, CalDAV, and the house’s own MCP server — and the four directions those connections can run in, with the conflict rules that make two-way survivable.

Ends with the four concrete suspects for why the iCal feed that already ships has never been seen to subscribe. All four are one-line fixes.

The one structural idea

A calendar is the only surface in the product where the past and the future are drawn in the same grid. Everywhere else the house looks at a record or at a projection; here it looks at both at once. So the design makes that the subject rather than hiding it: the future half of the month carries forecasts that name their issuer, the past half carries what happened, and each cell keeps the forecast it was given so the book scores itself. After ninety days the house has not just a covers model — it has evidence about whether to believe one.
