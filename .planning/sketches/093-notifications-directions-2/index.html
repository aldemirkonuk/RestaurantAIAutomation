The recommendation

Keep the day-book. Take direction A now, B when the new registers exist, C when the producers say what a row is about.

Why A now. It is the only one of the three that can be built today without either a fabrication or a bet. The day rail is not a decoration bolted onto the status quo: it is the shape the data already has — group_key is literally <rule>:<date>, one page a day for fifty days — and picking a day is a server-side read, so the number beside it is the register’s own. The founder liked the day strip and he was right about the dimension; the first pass was right that an hour axis is the wrong way to spend a page on it. A rail costs one row of the page and answers “what happened on Saturday”, which is a question the four new registers make real for the first time.

Why B is next and not now. A split is the correct answer to the volume the founder’s own new registers will create — a goal reached, every delivery, every invoice confirmation and every service record are all reports, and once they are written they will outnumber decisions by two orders of magnitude. But on today’s register, 641 of 663 rows are low stock and 631 are critical: room one would be the entire page and room two would hold four rows. Build the split the day the producers land, not before.

Why C is the one to want. It is the only direction that attacks what is actually wrong — the same sentence written 147 times, and nothing ever closing except by being read. It is also the only one that makes “subdue by settlement” natural. It is blocked on three columns that exist and are NULL on every row. That is a one-line change in each producer, and it is filed.

The strongest argument against, and why it loses

“A is the status quo with a widget on it. The founder said he was not sure about keeping; answering with ‘keep it, plus a date picker’ is the answer that takes the least courage, and it leaves his actual complaint — the page does not show enough of what is happening — exactly where it was. B is the structural change he was asking for. Build it now, and let the near-empty right-hand room be the visible argument for building the producers.”

That is the best case against, and it nearly wins. It loses on one thing: an empty room is not an argument, it is a claim. A page with “Needs a decision” full and “The house is telling you” holding four rows does not read as we have not built the producers yet. It reads as the house has nothing to report — a statement about the business made by a gap in our own instrumentation. That is the one fault this house has a standing rule against: a system reporting on itself reports absence as health. A rail over a single ledger makes no such claim; an empty day is visibly an empty day, and the register’s own total is printed beside it. The courage the counter-argument asks for would be spent on a structure that misleads until something else is fixed — and that something else is the same producer work under either answer.

The second-best argument against is smaller and is answered on the page itself: the rail’s little figures count only the rows already loaded, which is a half-truth. So they say “on this screen”, and the moment a day is picked the estimate is replaced by the register’s own count. The alternative — fourteen requests to fill the rail — costs more than it tells.
