The docket.

Each folio opens as a short ruled list of the acts it can take — as many as its register has lines — and each act's state column fills in as the fact lands: not yet answered, in pencil, posted with who and when, carried forward with its date, refused with the register's words. One act opens at a time and holds its lines and its control. The docket is the tutorial and the record in one object, so nothing about "what to do" can go stale.

Not yet answeredThe act is open. Its row opens to the lines and the control; nothing is assumed in the meantime.
In pencilMudavym heard or read it. The state says spoken or read, the time, and that it waits on the seal on fo. 5.
PostedDouble rule under the row; who, how (typed or confirmed) and when. The row still opens — to reopen as a correcting entry.
C/FOffered and carried forward, with its date. A recorded fact; the row offers to answer it now.
RefusedThe register's words under the state, verbatim. Nothing written; the row stays open with what you typed.
Opens whenThe act cannot be taken yet; the state names what unblocks it, and the row opens to that act instead.

What did Tuna tell you?

What the phone changes. The three columns stack into one — act, how, state — and the open row carries its field and its two acts. A manager holding a phone reads a list of what Tuna can tell him and sees, in the same list, what Tuna already has.

"Net 30" was spoken while Tuna was on the line; its row went to pencil with the time and the fo. 5 pointer. Delivery days posted the moment Hasan tapped them. The list never reorders itself — it is the vendor's terms in the order the register keeps them.

Six rows for one vendor: the docket is as long as the register's own lines, and that is the densest head of the three directions — every act is visible at once. The tab strip counts acts per folio (1/1, 0/2), a count of facts and never a percentage of the book.
