STATED — a person typed it, and their name and the date are kept. INFERRED — computed from the ledger at read time. Never written down as a fact. EM DASH — nobody has said and the ledger cannot tell. This is an answer, not a blank.
Why this belongs in settings and not in a vendor page. A cutoff is a rule the whole house obeys, like a unit or a sign-off — and once it is a setting, three other surfaces can read it. The calendar can draw “Anadolu closes in 3h 10m for Wednesday” as a real object on the day; the low-stock alert can say “order by 14:00 or it is Friday”; the order draft can refuse to promise Tuesday delivery from a vendor who does not deliver Tuesdays. The whole field stores cutoffs as inert settings text. Nobody draws them as time.

What this costs to build, precisely. No new table and no migration. system_audit_log carries actor_type · actor_id · action · entity_type · entity_id · changes jsonb · restaurant_id · created_at, and recordAccessChange is the shape two access changes already use. Three services need to capture a before-state they currently fetch and discard — feature flags, user preferences, notification preferences — and call it. One read endpoint renders this. The one trap: actor_id must be public.users.user_id, never an auth.users id; the two tables are disjoint in this database and a fresh CI database has no rows to catch the mistake. The last line of the row above then reads “changed · 3 days ago · by Selin Kara”, which is the whole point of the register.
